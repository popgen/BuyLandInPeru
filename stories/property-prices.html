Peru property prices: the data and the reality

What the numbers say, and why they understate what's happening in rural Peru.

Lucas
September 2026 · 3 min read

Peru's public property data tells a clear story for city apartments and almost nothing about rural land. Nationally, real (inflation-adjusted) prices have been roughly flat since 2020, while Lima's premium districts kept climbing. But the numbers that exist come from formal, urban listings, and in much of the country the real market is informal, under-reported, and moving faster than any dataset shows. This is the data alongside what it leaves out.

The national trend over the last ten years

Over roughly 2015 to 2025, average nominal home prices rose about 3% to 7% per year. After inflation, real gains are much more modest: close to flat since 2020. Prices actually dipped slightly in recent years, down about 1.5% nominally in 2024 (and more in real terms). The countrywide average sits around USD 1,200 per square meter, though that figure is dominated by urban apartments, especially in Lima.

The headline: as a national average, Peru real estate has been a modest, steady market lately, not a boom. The action is regional.

The spread within one area is the real story: in-town residential land near Tarapoto can cost 15 to 25 times more per square meter than a plot a few kilometers out. Rural Lamas land is cheaper still than Tarapoto proper.

Why the public numbers understate reality

Three things make the official data unreliable for rural Peru:

  1. The market is informal. There's no good central real estate portal. Many deals happen by word of mouth and over WhatsApp, and nothing gets reported to any tracker.
  2. Recorded prices are often below actual prices. To reduce the Alcabala transfer tax, sellers frequently record a lower official sale price and take the rest under the table. So even the deals that are documented understate what was really paid.
  3. Listings are asking prices, not sale prices, and sellers here tend to ask high.

The result: for rural and jungle land, there is essentially no reliable historical price series. On-the-ground knowledge is the only real source.

On the ground in Lamas and San Martín

What I see and hear locally paints a sharper picture than any dataset:

  • Rural land has jumped roughly fivefold since the pandemic. Around Lamas, a hectare that used to go for about 10,000 soles now runs closer to 50,000 soles. That kind of jump doesn't show up in any official series.
  • The fruit property: about 3.5 km outside Lamas, full of fruit trees, with water. A mid-range rural buy.
  • The homestead deal: 34 hectares (80+ acres) for 35,000 soles, just under USD 10,000 at the time. Far out, about a 1.5-hour hike in, raw land with no water or power. If you want lots of acreage to slowly build a homestead, deals like this exist, and you spend the savings on access and utilities.
  • Off-grid is doable. My own place runs on ten 680-watt solar panels, an inverter, and a large lithium battery. Raw land without utilities isn't a dealbreaker if you plan for solar.

Why demand outside the cities keeps growing

My read is that land slightly outside the cities will keep appreciating, driven by people looking for:

  • A hedge against inflation and economic uncertainty
  • Resilience against future pandemics and disruptions
  • The ability to grow their own food and raise their own animals
  • An escape from city pollution, light and noise

That's the same pull that brought me here, and I see more people feeling it every year. The affordable window on rural land is real, but it's narrowing. That's my opinion from living here, not a promise: land values can go down as well as up.
