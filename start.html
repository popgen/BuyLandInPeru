Figuring out what you want

The property is the last question, not the first.

People write me a neighborhood and a budget. The useful conversation starts earlier: how many months you will actually be here, what your body does in heat and altitude, and whether a cheap lot still looks cheap after a roof, a tank, and a road.

What I ask on an intake

The contact form is this list. Answer what you know. “Not sure” is a real answer.

  • Budget, and whether that number is the land only or the land plus a building.
  • How many months a year you expect to be in Peru.
  • Retirement, a vacation base, a rental, or a purchase you might sell. More than one can be true. None of them is a promise of profit.
  • Whether you intend to rent the place out, short stays or long.
  • Tolerance for heat, humidity, altitude, rain, noise, and isolation.

The life around the house

  • How close you need a real hospital, not a pharmacy with a red cross.
  • Airports: an international hub, a domestic flight, or a long road.
  • International schools, if children are part of the plan.
  • An expat scene, full immersion, or something in between.
  • Spanish: little, daily-life, or comfortable. Little Spanish is workable in parts of Lima and in a few beach towns. It is a hard way to buy land.
  • Internet for remote work. Fiber in town is a different fact from a rural tower or a satellite dish.

Altitude, before you fall in love with a photo

Many people do not think about this until the second night. I am not a doctor. If you have heart, lung, blood, or pregnancy concerns, talk to your own clinician before you plan a life above 2,000 meters.

Cusco

The historic center sits at about 3,400 meters. Headache, bad sleep, and shortness of breath are ordinary for new arrivals. A three-day tour is not a test of a year.

Arequipa

About 2,300 meters. Lower than Cusco, dry, and still high enough that some people feel it, especially with a heart or lung condition.

Huancayo

About 3,200 meters, in the central highlands. The city is practical and commercial. The altitude is not a footnote, and the road to Lima can close in the rains.

Puno

About 3,800 meters, on Lake Titicaca. This is among the highest cities buyers treat as a normal option. Cold is part of the day, not a surprise season.

The Sacred Valley is lower than Cusco’s plaza, often around 2,800 meters depending on the town. Lower is not the same as easy. Read the altitude essay and the region notes.

What “cheap property in Peru” looks like

A low number usually buys land, or an unfinished shell, or a house that still needs the unglamorous half of a house. Brick walls with no windows. A roof that leaks at the joint. Wiring that a cousin added. No registered building, only the land on the title. A lot that is cheap because the road dies in the rain, or because the seller cannot show a registered owner.

Common disappointments, in plain order:

  • The photo was taken at noon on a Saturday. Friday night is a discoteca, a church loudspeaker, or roosters.
  • The price was set for a foreign buyer. Local asking prices, in Spanish, tell a different story. I call that gringo pricing, and I compare against ranges I know.
  • The “house” is autoconstrucción: built without an engineer, in a country that has earthquakes.
  • The paper is a constancia de posesión, not a registered title.
  • The beach lot is beautiful and may sit inside the 50 km border strip, where foreigners generally cannot own without special authorization.

Building, renovating, and maintaining cost in kind, not in a number I am willing to print as fact. Labor from a maestro de obra is often the smaller surprise. Cement, steel, tile, a real electrical install, a cistern, and a roof that survives a hard rain are the larger ones. Jungle buildings also fight humidity and termites. A simple extra room can be a modest local project. A finished house with reliable water and decent wiring is a different project. Get written quotes for the specific plan. Treat any dollar figure in a Facebook group as a rumor.

Rent in the area before you buy

Many people should live in a place for a few months before they own anything there. January and July are different countries in the same valley: rain, fog, tourists, empty streets, water pressure. A rental shows you the hospital road, the market, and whether you sleep.

I can help arrange that. It is a smaller piece of work than a purchase, and it is often the one that saves the purchase. Say so on the intake if you want a rental first.
