Region guides

Where to buy land in Peru: eleven regions, honestly

How each place behaves day to day: climate, food, pace, and the water, power, internet, roads and hospitals that listings skip. I live in the high jungle and know it best; the rest are practical notes, not claims that I've lived in every city. Schedules, roads and hospitals change, so confirm details once a town is on your short list.

Climate and ground that listings skip

Garúa

Lima's winter, roughly May through October, is gray drizzle and damp cloud, not a rainy season. Houses feel cold because they're built for summer. Summer, roughly December through April, is when the sky opens and the coast shows you why people move there.

Jungle roads

In the selva, the rainy months can turn a "one hour" track into a walk. If you'll need the property in February, see it in February, or accept that you haven't really seen it.

El Niño

The north coast floods in strong El Niño years. Rivers move, beach towns take damage, and one dry January proves nothing about the next. Nobody can predict the cycle, but we can check whether a lot sits in a flood path.

Huaicos

In the Andes, a dry ravine in July can run with mud and rock in the rains. Don't buy the pretty floor of a quebrada because the day you visited was sunny.

Earthquakes

Peru sits on a subduction coast. Seismic risk is normal, especially near the ocean, and it isn't zero in the highlands. How a building was made matters as much as the view. Informal autoconstrucción is common, so whether an engineer was involved is something to check, not assume.

High jungle · heat · rain

Selva alta: Tarapoto, Lamas, MoyobambaWhere I live

This is where I live, so I'll be upfront that I know it best. It's the high jungle, not the floodplain of Iquitos. Tarapoto is the hub: mototaxis, heat, a real town, and weekends pointed at rivers and waterfalls. Lamas sits higher and a little cooler, a smaller place with a strong identity. Moyobamba is greener still, orchid country, and slower. All three are hot and humid by coastal standards. Rain is a season and also a habit.

Food is plantain, juane, tacacho, smoked meat and river fish. The pace is unhurried until you're in a mototaxi. Spanish is how everything gets done. Safety is small-city: ordinary caution, and more care with anything that looks like a flash of cash. Costs are generally kinder than Lima or Cusco's tourist core. Private hospital care is regional, and complicated medicine means a flight.

Tarapoto has a commercial airport with daily flights from Lima. The road from the coast is a long mountain crossing, not an afternoon errand. Rural land around these towns is where the affordable acreage is: around Lamas, a hectare that sold for about 10,000 soles before the pandemic now goes for closer to 50,000. Title, access in the rain, and who the community says owns the land are the real purchase.

A day there

You're sweaty before breakfast, so the useful hours are early. Mototaxis are the bus system. Lunch is heavy and welcome. Shutters close against the glare. When it rains, it rains as an event, and the street runs with water. Evening is when people sit outside, because the walls hold the day's heat. Insects are real here: a cheap house that ignores termites will show you the bill in the wood.

Infrastructure

WaterUrban networks in the towns, and tanks everywhere. Rural systems can run only some hours a day, and water lines do break.
PowerTown grids are real, and storms cut them. A rural lot may mean solar or a generator. My own place runs on ten 680-watt panels, an inverter and a lithium battery.
InternetFiber in central Tarapoto and parts of the other towns. On rural land it's mobile data or satellite, and only while the power lasts.
RoadsPaved main roads, and side roads that change personality in the rains. See a place in the wet season if you'll need to use it then.
Hospital and airportTarapoto has both. Lamas and Moyobamba add road time.

Cloud forest · cooler · rain

Oxapampa

Oxapampa is still selva alta, but it doesn't feel like Tarapoto. The valley is higher, often around 1,800 meters, with cloud, coffee, dairy, and a town whose founders came from central Europe. Pozuzo is the smaller relative down the road. It's cooler, wetter and greener, and mold is a housing issue, not a housekeeping insult.

Food leans to dairy, river trout, and a mix of highland and jungle plates. The pace is a provincial valley. Costs are moderate. Health care is a local hospital at provincial scale, and Lima is a long road, not a quick flight.

A day there

Mist in the morning, or a hard rain that makes the decision for you. You walk to a bakery or the market in a jacket. People are doing farm and town errands. By afternoon you know whether the road you need is the road you have. Nights are quiet off the highway, and damp enough that a badly built closet becomes a science experiment.

Infrastructure

WaterTown supply plus a lot of rain. Drainage matters more than scarcity.
PowerConnected in town, with storm cuts. Rural valleys vary.
InternetPossible in town, optimistic on a hillside. Satellite is the realistic backup for remote work.
RoadsThe road from Lima is long and foggy, and it's the reason people either commit or don't. Rain makes it longer.
Hospital and airportA local hospital for ordinary care. Serious care and flights mean Lima or another city, by road first.

Amazon · river and air · no road out

Iquitos

Iquitos is a city on the river. You arrive by air or by water, and there's no road connecting it to the rest of Peru. That one fact organizes everything else: freight, visitors, a medical emergency, a bag of cement. The climate is hot and wet, steadily. Flood season changes which streets and which stilt houses are standing in water.

Food is Amazonian: plantain, river fish, juane, and fruit that doesn't ship well. It's a real city, with neighborhoods, universities and noise. Cost of living looks low until you import a habit, because almost everything not local came by boat or plane. Parts of Loreto lie inside the 50 km border zone even when the city itself is the draw, so the exact lot has to be measured.

A day there

Heat from the start. A motocarro to the market. The river is commerce, not scenery, for the people using it. Rain arrives as a wall and then the street steams. You drink cold things and don't schedule a walk at noon. If you live on the flood edge, the season decides which floor you're using.

Infrastructure

WaterUrban service exists and people still store water. Quality and continuity need testing house by house.
PowerThe city is electrified, and outages are why everyone has backup batteries.
InternetUsable in much of the city, frustrating in a downpour, and a project outside it.
RoadsInside the city only, plus rivers.
Hospital and airportBoth in the city. The airport is the exit, and that's the whole transport plan.

Coast · sea level

Lima

Lima is a string of districts with different prices, cliffs and commutes, not one neighborhood called Lima. Miraflores, Barranco and San Isidro are the names foreigners already know: more services, higher prices, more English in cafés, and more traffic and tourism. Inland districts can be quieter and cheaper, though "cheaper" in Lima still isn't a small lot for a few thousand dollars.

The climate is mild and nearly rainless, with the gray garúa winter. Food is the country's deep end: markets, set-lunch menús, ceviche, and everything in between. Safety is uneven by district and by hour; petty theft on streets and in taxis is the everyday problem. Cost of living is the highest on this page, and it can still be moderate if you shop the way residents do. Private health care is the best in the country, and Jorge Chávez is the international airport.

A day there

You wake to flat gray light for much of the year. If you work online, the question is the cable in the building, not the weather. Lunch is still the main meal. The ocean is close only if your district actually touches it, and many don't. Evening cools off. The noise is buses and horns, or the malecón if you paid for it. In summer the gray lifts and the parks fill.

Infrastructure

WaterFormal districts are on the network. Many houses still keep a tank, because pressure isn't guaranteed.
PowerGenerally stable in established neighborhoods. Ask about the building, not the city.
InternetFiber is common in established districts and missing on newer informal edges.
RoadsTraffic is the constraint, rarely rain. The building's seismic design is the real ground question.
Hospital and airportMajor private hospitals are in the city. The airport is in Callao, and the drive depends on traffic.

Coast · heat · El Niño

Northern beaches: Máncora, Punta Sal, Huanchaco

These aren't the same town. Máncora, in Piura, is the known surf and party strip: loud nights in season, a sleepy beach town out of it. Punta Sal, in Tumbes, is quieter and closer to Ecuador. Huanchaco, next to Trujillo, is a working beach town with a fishing tradition and more everyday life.

Heat is the climate. Food is seafood and ceviche. Day-to-day prices sit below Lima's beach districts. A clinic in a beach village isn't a hospital; serious care is in Piura, Tumbes or Trujillo, and those distances matter at night. The far north is also where the 50 km border rule starts to matter, so a lawyer needs to check the exact coordinates before you get attached.

A day there

Heat by mid-morning, so errands happen early. Lunch is the long part of the day, often fish. In Máncora in season, afternoons are the beach and nights are music you didn't ask for. In Huanchaco the boats and the town carry on either way. In a strong rain year the question isn't the sunset; it's where the water went on the street behind the house.

Infrastructure

WaterTown systems exist and strain in season. A tank is normal.
PowerConnected in town. Storms and informal edges are less reliable.
InternetWorkable in town centers. Don't assume a beach lot has fiber.
RoadsThe Pan-American Highway is the spine, and El Niño years damage it.
Hospital and airportIn the regional cities, not on the sand. Check the night drive, not the map distance.

South · about 2,300 m · dry

Arequipa

Arequipa is a real city with a walkable center, volcanoes on the horizon, and a pride you can taste in the food. Days are often sunny and sharp, nights cool off, and the rainy season is shorter and lighter than Cusco's. Rocoto relleno, adobo and a long lunch in a picantería are everyday food, not a show for visitors.

Cost of living is easier than central Lima. Private clinics and a serious hospital network make it the medical hub of the south, and the airport has commercial flights. At about 2,300 meters some people feel nothing, and some people, especially with heart or lung conditions, should talk to a doctor first.

A day there

Sun on stone in the morning. You walk for bread or to a market. Work happens indoors because the noon light is strong. Lunch is hot food and a long pause. Late afternoon is errands before the temperature drops. In the wet weeks the sky finally acts like weather, then goes back to clear.

Infrastructure

WaterUrban network in the city. Pressure varies, and tanks are common.
PowerReliable by provincial standards.
InternetFiber in much of the city, thinner at the edges and in the countryside.
RoadsThe city is drivable. Seismic construction still matters.
Hospital and airportBoth in the city, which is why smaller southern towns treat Arequipa as the backup plan.

Andes · about 3,400 m in the center

Cusco

The center is beautiful, and it's a tourist machine. Neighborhoods away from the plaza are where a life would actually happen, at a very different noise level. The dry months, roughly May to August, are clear with cold nights. The rainy months bring afternoon storms and green hills. Market food is soup, corn and a set lunch; plaza restaurants are priced for someone leaving on Thursday. Property is priced the same split way.

There are clinics and a regional hospital, but complicated cases and serious altitude sickness usually end up in Lima. The airport is in the city, and the landing itself is your first altitude test. If you want to live here, plan a long stay before you buy. A weekend is how people make the mistake.

A day there

Mornings are cold and bright in the dry season, or cold and wet in the rains. You walk slowly because the air is thinner, even after you think you've adjusted. Coffee, then a market. Lunch is the meal that keeps people upright. Evenings are layers. In the center you hear bars; on a residential street, a television and a dog.

Infrastructure

WaterCity network, with the usual tank habit. Hillside settlements are less formal.
PowerAdequate in town. Storms interrupt more than on the dry coast.
InternetFiber exists in town. The valley and the hills are another conversation.
RoadsRain, landslides and festival crowds. The airport road isn't the road to a rural lot.
Hospital and airportBoth in the city. Evacuation to Lima is the plan for anything worse.

Andes · lower than Cusco, still high

The Sacred Valley

Urubamba, Yucay, Pisac, Ollantaytambo and the towns between them are farming valleys with tourism layered on top. Elevations are often around 2,800 meters. That's kinder than Cusco's plaza and still a high valley. The river is the beauty and the risk: a flat green field can be a flood field.

The pace is tractors and school runs, plus tour buses at the famous spots. English shows up among foreign owners and in restaurants, but daily life with neighbors is in Spanish. Costs sit in a wide band: a local house is one market, and a view sold to foreigners is another. Clinics are limited; Cusco is the hospital trip, and Lima the next one. Ask who actually holds title.

A day there

Light on the fields early, and the sound of animals or a workshop. The town handles school and market in the morning. Midday is hot in the sun and cool in the shade. On an ordinary Wednesday people are working. In the rains the river looks more like a river, and some side roads become a reason to stay home.

Infrastructure

WaterTown systems and irrigation exist. A house may still depend on a tank, a spring or a neighbor's ditch. Ask which.
PowerTowns are connected. Outlying parcels may not be.
InternetImproving in the towns. A field usually means mobile data or satellite.
RoadsThe valley road is the spine. Side access and flood paths need a visit, not a pin on a map.
Hospital and airportCusco for both. Know the night drive.

Northern highlands · about 2,700 m

Cajamarca

Cajamarca is a highland city of dairy farms, stone, carnival, and a colder rain than the coast expects. About 2,700 meters is enough to respect and much easier than Puno. The wet season is green and muddy, which is why rural roads need a second look.

Food is cheese, manjar, potatoes and a hot table. The pace is provincial and proud, not an expat scene. Costs are generally friendly. Health care is a regional hospital, and specialty care often means Trujillo or Lima. The airport makes it more reachable than the map suggests.

A day there

A cold morning and a jacket. The market is the event. Outdoor work happens while the light is up, because rain in season shortens the day. Lunch is filling, and people know each other. In carnival season the city isn't trying to be convenient.

Infrastructure

WaterCity network, rural springs and ditches. Check storage at the specific house.
PowerNormal in the city. Countryside lines trip in storms.
InternetFine for many city addresses. A farm is a test, not an assumption.
RoadsRain and mountain grades.
Hospital and airportBoth serve the city.

Central highlands · about 3,200 m

Huancayo

Huancayo is a commercial highland city in the Mantaro Valley, about 3,200 meters up. People come for trade, the Sunday fair, the universities, and a valley that's more lived-in than photographed. Nights are cold, and in the rainy season huaicos and closures on the road to Lima stop being theoretical.

Food is highland: potatoes, pachamanca, and serious soup. The pace is business, not resort. Costs are moderate. Health care is regional, and air connections are limited compared with Cusco or Arequipa.

A day there

Cold air, then a city that's already selling something. You dress in layers you'll shed by late morning if the sun is out. Lunch resets the altitude. In the rains you watch the hills, not just the sky, because the danger is often a slope above a road.

Infrastructure

WaterUrban service with the highland tank habit. Rural canals are a separate legal question if you're buying fields.
PowerCity grid. Rural lines are less steady.
InternetUsable in the city for most work. Test the specific street.
RoadsThe road to Lima is famous and interruptible.
Hospital and airportA regional hospital in the city. Lima is the serious medical and flight connection.

Altiplano · about 3,800 m · border country

Puno

Puno is the high lake. About 3,800 meters is the daily fact: cold, thin air, strong sun, and a cultural calendar that isn't arranged for newcomers. Juliaca, nearby, is the commercial and airport town. Bolivia is close enough that any rural purchase needs the 50 km border question answered with coordinates.

Food is quinoa, trout and potatoes. Many families speak Aymara or Quechua alongside Spanish. Costs are generally lower than Cusco's visitor economy. Health care is regional, and altitude illness is not a quaint first-day story at this height. Talk to a doctor before planning a life here.

A day there

You wake cold. The sun, when it arrives, is strong, and it doesn't mean the air got thicker. Walking uphill is a meeting with your lungs. The lake is enormous and the wind comes off it. At night the temperature drops hard. If you arrived from the coast last Tuesday, this isn't the week to buy.

Infrastructure

WaterCity systems. Check storage and what a rural parcel actually receives.
PowerUrban grid. The rural altiplano varies.
InternetWorkable in town. A lakeshore field is a separate test.
RoadsRain, altitude and distance. Festival weeks fill transport.
Hospital and airportRegional care in Puno, flights from Juliaca, and Lima for serious medicine.
